SQLingo

A mobile-first, gamified practice app for SQL. People watch hours of tutorials but never get feedback on whether they can write the query. SQLingo gives them short activities with instant validation and a reason to come back tomorrow. Built solo, with no developer and no budget.

Read the full case study Try it live
Product story
Problem
Learners consume SQL content but have no practice environment and no feedback loop. Live classes cost ₹5,000–7,000, hard to justify before you know SQL is for you.
User
Freshers building a data analytics resume, and working professionals who want to pull their own data without waiting on a tech team.
Insight
The gap isn't content, it's feedback. It started with a student who had learned SQL by writing it in a notebook, with no way to check an answer.
My role
Founder and PM: discovery, personas, competitor analysis, scope, design system, content architecture, testing and deployment. Built with AI-assisted development.
Product decisions
  • No free-type SQL in lessons. Structured activities give instant, unambiguous validation.
  • Freemium: free lessons, with AI-powered mock interviews planned as the paid tier.
  • Mistake-retry queue: wrong answers return until they are answered correctly.
  • Module tests (20 questions, 80% to pass). A reference button added after user feedback.
  • I chose warm teal and the Nunito and JetBrains Mono typefaces with accessibility in mind, and no mascot, so the brand stays portable.
  • I used Sakila, a real open-source database, so learners practise on realistic table structures.
What shipped
Three modules, 13 lessons and 105 activities, with XP, streaks and mistake retry. Separate test and production environments.
Trade-offs
Single-file front end with no backend. Progress saves in the browser only; login, leaderboards and cross-device sync are not built yet.
Outcome
Live in production. A first external user, a complete SQL beginner, tested it and three pieces of feedback were actioned.

PM skills shown: discovery, MVP scoping, prioritisation, monetisation thinking, user feedback loops, QA.

AI Student Support Chatbot

An internal AI support product built from historical support interactions, with explicit response guardrails and QA before launch. It supported student queries when the technical team was unavailable, helping with navigation and concept clarification while refusing to provide direct answers to assignments or quizzes.

Product story
Problem
Student questions came in when the tech team wasn't available, and the support and operations teams couldn't answer them.
User
Students asking for help, and the support and operations teams who fielded those questions.
Insight
Past student–teacher email exchanges already held the answers to recurring questions, so they became the training data.
My role
Product Owner and QA lead. Built with an intern; I owned the requirements, the guardrail rules and the testing.
Product decisions
  • Guardrail: help with navigation and concept clarification, never give direct answers to assignments or quizzes.
  • Fine-tune on the company's own historical exchanges instead of relying on a generic model.
Quality and risk
Tested responses against the guardrail rules before launch.
Outcome
Launched to support student queries.

PM skills shown: AI product ownership, guardrail design, QA, risk management, working with support and operations teams.

Background

Where I'm coming from.

I work across product, analytics and business analysis. My background includes product roles at GreyCampus and Quantumics.AI, and I currently work as a Business Analyst at Cognizant, with client Apple on Apple Maps Analytics.

Data is part of how I work: SQL, BI, product analytics and reporting. I use it to understand product and business problems, not as an end in itself.

Alongside my professional roles, I continue to build and test products independently.

About

The thread through my work.

I trained as a pharmacist, then moved into data: first teaching it, then using it in product roles. Teaching SQL, Python, Power BI and Tableau was the most direct user research I could have asked for. I watched where people got stuck, what they asked three times, and what made them quit.

My work has spanned learning products, AI tools, analytics and enterprise reporting, and the pattern is the same each time. I find where users or the business are getting stuck, reduce the ambiguity, use data to test the problem, and decide what is worth building. Then I ship something small and see what happens.

That is why I keep building and testing products of my own.
